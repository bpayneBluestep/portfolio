Where I speak

I present regularly at NATSAP, the national association for therapeutic schools and programs, on technology, design and behavioral healthcare. I get asked to sit on industry panels and to consult with program teams working through their own technology decisions.

The talks change, but the subject rarely does: start with the people and the workflow, and the software gets a lot easier to get right. I've been the user, the builder and the executive, and I still think the user is the one worth listening to.

Featured talk

Transforming Treatment: A Guide in Using Technology to Improve Everything. Co-presented with Josh Gardner, Havenwood Academy.

Six case studies from Havenwood Academy and Alpine Academy, each told the same way: the pain points, the likely causes, and the layers of technology and process we added until the problem went away. Parents blowing up at therapists. Shifts that ran differently depending on who was on. Family phone calls that kept getting missed. Departments that never saw each other's notes.

Technology is Swiss cheese
No single tool fixes a problem. Each layer has holes; stack enough of them and nothing gets through.
What you chart on is what you care about
When a shift gets a visible grade, the shift gets better. The Hawthorne effect is a design tool.
Just-in-time information
The right information, to the right person, at the right time and place, in the right format. Silos break down when nobody has to go looking.
Pick the right tools
The best-equipped programs win, but nobody needs an excavator for a home garden.

It closes with an open debate on AI in behavioral health: what it is actually good for today, where it isn't, and what programs want vendors like BlueStep to build next.
