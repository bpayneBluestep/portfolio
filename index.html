I started as a BlueStep user at a treatment program in southern Utah. Today I help run the company. This is how that happened.

2019

Director of Growth, Havenwood Academy, Cedar City, Utah

The user

I spent three years at Havenwood Academy, a residential treatment program in Cedar City, and finished as Director of Growth on the leadership team after my MBA. Everything there ran on BlueStep: clinical notes, admissions, the census, the reports leadership read every Monday morning.

And like most software at most treatment programs, it did some of what people needed while staff worked around the rest. I started fixing the rest. Nobody asked me to. I taught myself to build on the platform one problem at a time: a report a director couldn't get, a form that ate a therapist's afternoon.

By the time I was running growth, I was also the person on campus who customized BlueStep. Doing both taught me that the most important problems in a business are rarely software problems first, but software is usually how you fix them for good. I never learned software from the vendor's side. I learned it from the people who had to use it.

In 2022 I also joined the founding team of Engage, a new young adult program, and designed and built its first website before it served its first student.

2022

VP of Behavioral Healthcare, Xantie Analytics

The builder

Xantie, a data consulting firm in Salt Lake City, hired me to run its behavioral healthcare practice. The practice was me and one anchor analytics contract. Three months in, that contract ended.

I could have gone looking for more consulting. I bet on product instead. As a one-person business unit I owned sales, product, engineering, customer success and accounts, and built two products that I sold directly to residential treatment centers:

  • An admissions CRM built for how programs actually work: families, referral partners, and a pipeline that runs on relationships.
  • An outcomes platform that schedules every patient's assessments (PHQ-9, YOQ or a facility's own surveys) from their admit and discharge dates, then shows clinicians whether treatment is working.

Both ran on top of BlueStep's own platform. And both did the job better than BlueStep's native tools.

2025

BlueStep Systems acquires Xantie's behavioral healthcare business

The software I started customizing as a user bought the software I built on top of it.

In early 2025 BlueStep acquired the business, and I joined as Executive Director: the first dedicated executive operator in the company's twenty-year history.

Now

Executive Director, BlueStep Systems

The rebuild

BlueStep had been building each client's system by hand. That kept margins razor-thin and made every new customer a custom project. Over twelve months we rebuilt it into a modular suite that ships ready to use and gets configured, not coded, for each program.

I led that rebuild and wrote roughly half of its code myself while running the day-to-day business: pricing, hiring, implementations and the P&L. Onboarding got faster, per-client engineering dropped, and the scaling ceiling that had capped growth for years went away.

Some of what shipped along the way:

  • Telehealth integrations running at more than twenty facilities
  • The first insurance claim submitted straight from the platform to a clearinghouse
  • Native dashboards that replaced paid Power BI embeds
  • An in-house survey tool that replaced a paid Typeform subscription
  • A drag-and-drop document manager on every client and lead record
  • Treatment targets that score progress inside every session note

Notable projects

First, what BlueStep is

BlueStep is the software that runs behavioral health programs: residential treatment centers, therapeutic boarding schools and young adult programs, where teenagers and young adults live for months while they get treatment.

A program like that is a hospital, a school, a dorm and a business at once. BlueStep is the system all of it runs on: the clinical chart, admissions, the school, billing, staffing and the portal parents log into. Every program is different, so every part of it has to be configurable.

Program
A residential treatment center or therapeutic school. Our customers.
Direct-care staff
The people on shift with residents day and night. Not clinicians, and rarely at a desk.
Shift note
What staff write at the end of every shift about each resident. It becomes part of the medical record.
Milieu
The daily living environment of the program: meals, school, groups, free time.
Educational consultant
An independent advisor families hire to find the right program for their child. The biggest source of referrals in the industry.

BlueIQ

BlueStep's AI product line. I designed and built every feature.

Direct-care staff spend their shifts on their feet with residents, then sit down at the end and type notes that become a medical record. Clinicians and directors then read hundreds of those notes to figure out what actually happened.

BlueIQ puts AI where that time goes. Staff talk instead of type, and leadership gets the picture without reading everything.

  • Record with BlueIQ. Staff dictate in the browser, and the note comes back in the behavior, intervention and response structure that insurers look for. The model is told to never add anything the staff member didn't say.
  • An assistant that answers from the chart. Ask about one resident or the whole program and get answers with citations back to the records they came from. It runs as the person asking, so it can only see what they're allowed to see.
  • The daily milieu summary. Every morning at 5, leadership gets a plain-language recap of the previous day, built from the sources each program chooses.
  • Utilization review drafts. The narrative sections insurers require get drafted from the clinical record, so clinicians edit instead of starting from a blank page.

Under the hood: per-program usage credits so AI cost is metered, and a move from our own OpenAI integration to the platform's native AI once it shipped, with OpenAI kept as a fallback.

The referral marketplace

Connecting educational consultants and programs. In design.

Educational consultants place families with programs. Programs live on those referrals. But each side tracks the relationship in its own CRM, and everything between them happens over phone calls and email: who has a bed open, who fits this kid, how the last placement turned out.

I'm designing the layer that connects them. Consultants get a CRM built for how they work, programs keep theirs, and the referral itself becomes a shared record that both sides can see and act on. Openings and outcomes flow back, so consultants can send families to programs with real evidence.

The hard part is the same as in any two-sided product: each side needs its own view of the same truth, and neither should see what isn't theirs.

One product, many programs

The platform rebuild and how it ships.

BlueStep used to build every customer's system by hand. Each new program was a custom project, margins were thin, and a fix for one customer never reached the others.

We rebuilt it as one modular product. Each program turns features on or off and sets up its own roles, so a single change to a role updates around fifty permissions for everyone who holds it.

Then I built how it ships. Changes are made once in a source system and rolled out by script to every production customer, with a test customer first and a separate read-only check afterward that confirms each one actually landed.

Agreements

E-signatures, built into the platform.

Admissions runs on signatures: consent forms, financial agreements, releases. Programs were paying for a separate e-signature service and then copying signed documents back into the chart by hand.

I built the whole flow inside BlueStep: a template studio where programs place signature fields, signing from inside the record or from a public link sent to parents, and a background job that files every completed agreement where it belongs.

It went out to eight customers at once, and every record was checked afterward. Nothing failed without someone knowing.

The application builder

Admissions forms programs design themselves.

Every program wants its own admissions application, and every change used to mean an engineer. Then someone retyped the answers into the chart.

Programs now build their applications with drag and drop, including questions that appear only when an earlier answer calls for them. Families fill them out on a public site that won't accept the same submission twice and confirms by email. Every answer maps into the right place in the chart automatically.

Incident compliance dashboard

Seeing patterns across a whole program.

Programs document every incident, but leadership couldn't see the patterns: which teams, which times of day, which kinds of incidents were rising.

I designed a dashboard that shows trends against the previous period, team scorecards, incidents by category, and a heatmap of day of week against hour. Every chart has a table view behind it, and it can all be exported.

While building it, I found duplicate records that had been inflating every number by about 12%. A dashboard is only as honest as its data.
