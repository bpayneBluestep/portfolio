I started as a BlueStep user at a treatment program in southern Utah. Today I help run the company. This is how that happened.

2019

Director of Growth, Havenwood Academy, Cedar City, Utah

The user

I spent three years at Havenwood Academy, a residential treatment program in Cedar City, and finished as Director of Growth on the leadership team after my MBA. Everything there ran on BlueStep: clinical notes, admissions, the census, the reports leadership read every Monday morning.

And like most software at most treatment programs, it did some of what people needed while staff worked around the rest. I started fixing the rest. Nobody asked me to. I taught myself to build on the platform one problem at a time: a report a director couldn't get, a form that ate a therapist's afternoon.

By the time I was running growth, I was also the person on campus who customized BlueStep. Doing both taught me that the most important problems in a business are rarely software problems first, but software is usually how you fix them for good. I never learned software from the vendor's side. I learned it from the people who had to use it.

In 2022 I also joined the founding team of Engage, a new young adult program, and designed and built its first website before it served its first student.

2022

VP of Behavioral Healthcare, Xantie Analytics

The builder

Xantie hired me to run its behavioral healthcare practice. The practice was me and one analytics contract. Three months in, that contract ended. I bet on product instead of more consulting.

As a one-person business unit I owned sales, product, engineering, customer success and accounts, and built two products that I sold directly to residential treatment centers.

An admissions CRM

Built for how programs actually work: families, referral partners, and a pipeline that runs on relationships.

An outcomes platform

Schedules every patient's assessments (PHQ-9, YOQ or a facility's own surveys) from their admit and discharge dates, then shows clinicians whether treatment is working.

Both ran on BlueStep's own platform, and both did the job better than BlueStep's native tools.

2025. BlueStep Systems acquires Xantie's behavioral healthcare business.

The software I started customizing as a user bought the software I built on top of it.

I joined as Executive Director: the first dedicated executive operator in the company's twenty-year history.

Now. Executive Director, BlueStep Systems

The rebuild

BlueStep had been building each client's system by hand. That kept margins razor-thin and made every new customer a custom project. Over twelve months we rebuilt it into a modular suite that ships ready to use and gets configured, not coded, for each program.

I led that rebuild and wrote roughly half of its code myself while running the day-to-day business: pricing, hiring, implementations and the P&L. Onboarding got faster, per-client engineering dropped, and the scaling ceiling that had capped growth for years went away.

Some of what shipped along the way

  • Telehealth integrations running at more than twenty facilities
  • The first insurance claim submitted straight from the platform to a clearinghouse
  • Native dashboards that replaced paid Power BI embeds
  • An in-house survey tool that replaced a paid Typeform subscription
  • A drag-and-drop document manager on every client and lead record
  • Treatment targets that score progress inside every session note

Notable projects

First, what BlueStep is

BlueStep runs the programs where teenagers and young adults live for months while they get treatment. Each one is a hospital, a school, a dorm and a business at once.

BlueStep is the system all of it runs on: the clinical chart, admissions, the school, billing, staffing and the portal parents log into. Every program is different, so every part of it has to be configurable. A few terms you'll see below:

Program
A residential treatment center or therapeutic school. Our customers.
Direct-care staff
The people on shift with residents day and night. Rarely at a desk.
Shift note
What staff write about each resident every shift. It becomes part of the medical record.
Milieu
The daily living environment: meals, school, groups, free time.
Educational consultant
An advisor families hire to find the right program. The industry's biggest source of referrals.

BlueIQ

BlueStep's AI product line. I designed and built every feature.

Direct-care staff spend their shifts on their feet with residents, then sit down at the end and type notes that become a medical record. Clinicians and directors then read hundreds of those notes to figure out what actually happened. BlueIQ puts AI where that time goes.

Record with BlueIQ

Staff dictate in the browser, and the note comes back in the behavior, intervention and response structure insurers look for. The model is told never to add anything the staff member didn't say.

An assistant that answers from the chart

Ask about one resident or the whole program and get answers with citations back to the records. It runs as the person asking, so it only sees what they're allowed to see.

The daily milieu summary

Every morning at 5, leadership gets a plain-language recap of the previous day, built from the sources each program chooses.

Utilization review drafts

The narrative sections insurers require get drafted from the clinical record, so clinicians edit instead of starting from a blank page.

Under the hood: per-program usage credits so AI cost is metered, and a move from our own OpenAI integration to the platform's native AI once it shipped, with OpenAI kept as a fallback.

In design

The referral marketplace

Educational consultants place families with programs, and programs live on those referrals. But each side tracks the relationship in its own CRM, and everything between them happens over phone calls and email: who has a bed open, who fits this kid, how the last placement turned out.

I'm designing the layer that connects them. The referral becomes a shared record both sides can see and act on, and openings and outcomes flow back so consultants can place families with real evidence.

The hard part is the same as in any two-sided product: each side needs its own view of the same truth, and neither should see what isn't theirs.

One product, many programs

The platform rebuild and how it ships

Each program turns features on or off and sets up its own roles; one change to a role updates around fifty permissions for everyone who holds it. Changes are made once and rolled out by script to every customer, with a test customer first and a read-only check afterward that confirms each one landed.

Agreements

E-signatures, built into the platform

Programs were paying for a separate e-signature service, then copying signed documents into the chart by hand. Now there's a template studio, signing inside the record or from a link sent to parents, and a background job that files every completed agreement. It went out to eight customers at once with nothing failing silently.

The application builder

Admissions forms programs design themselves

Every change to an admissions application used to mean an engineer, and then someone retyped the answers. Programs now build applications with drag and drop and conditional questions; families submit on a public site that rejects duplicates, and every answer maps into the chart automatically.

Incident compliance dashboard

Seeing patterns across a whole program

Trends against the previous period, team scorecards, categories, and a heatmap of day of week against hour, with a table view behind every chart. While building it I found duplicate records that had been inflating every number by about 12%. A dashboard is only as honest as its data.
